P2P AI-Assisted Micro-Task Settlement DRAFT v2.0

A peer-to-peer task dispatch and settlement protocol for sudowork, built on nexus-vfs primitives.
Your AI copilot matches, executes, and settles — from video editing to data labeling.

Why this matters

1. The opportunity

This is not just a gig platform. AI copilots make creative work — video editing, content creation — accessible to anyone. A university student equipped with sudowork's editing skill can produce professional-quality short-form video in 30 minutes. At ¥80-150 per video, the effective hourly rate is ¥160-300/h — exceeding tutoring, food delivery, and every other student gig option in China. This is AI-assisted labor that is already competitive on pure cash income, before any token leverage.

2. Value to sudowork's existing collaboration network

P2P task settlement adds an economic incentive layer to sudowork's cross-org collaboration network. Today, sudowork enables multi-agent collaboration across companies and domains, but open-source contributors, freelance experts, and cross-org working groups collaborate without shared payroll and have no native way to settle contributions. P2P settlement turns "we can work together" into "we can work together and settle payment trustlessly", compounding the network effect — every new participant is both a collaboration partner and a potential buyer or seller.

3. Agent-native vs platform-mediated

Traditional platforms (RTB, gig marketplaces) centralize matching power and take a large rake. Agent-native matching is fundamentally different:

DimensionPlatform-mediated (traditional)Agent-native (sudowork)
Matching powerCentralized exchange decidesBuyer & seller agents negotiate directly (P2P)
Task understandingPlatform doesn't understand task contentAgent decomposes, verifies, and executes with full content access
Verification3rd-party platform, limited visibilityLocal agent verifies within L1 trust zone
Seller learningNone — seller is a passive slotAgent learns seller preferences over time, becomes a better advocate

The seller's agent learns preferences over time. Unlike platform matching where every task is independent, sudowork accumulates knowledge about its human: what tasks they excel at, preferred working hours, quality patterns, domain expertise. This local learning (entirely within L1) means task selection improves with use.

LayerWhoTrust LevelDesign Implication
L1: My Trust Zone Me ↔ My sudowork Complete Runs locally, acts 100% in my interest. I paid for the points, my AI is my agent. All secrets, strategies, acceptance logic stay local.
L2: Peer Zone My sudowork ↔ Their sudowork Zero Counterparty could cheat. Need cryptographic signatures, hash commitments. Every message must be verifiable. No shared state without proof.

Design Principle

Minimize L3 (server) involvement. Maximize L1 (local) intelligence. Make L2 (peer) interactions cryptographically verifiable. The server is a witness, not an arbiter.

3. AI as Advocate (Trust Zone Alignment)

Interest Alignment Principle

Each sudowork instance is fully aligned with its human's interest. The human paid real money for the points powering that sudowork — so the AI and the human are economically bound.

  • Buyer's sudowork: optimizes for quality verification, fair pricing, detecting incomplete work
  • Seller's sudowork: optimizes for meeting acceptance criteria efficiently, advocating for fair compensation, protecting against unreasonable rejection

This adversarial alignment is by design — it produces fair outcomes the same way buyer/seller negotiation does in real markets.

Seller-Initiated Contracts (reverse dispatch)

The default flow is buyer-initiated: buyer posts a task, seller accepts. But the protocol also supports seller-initiated contracts, where the seller proposes work and finds a buyer.

Example: An engineer at a company spots a bug and proposes: "I'll fix this authentication issue, estimated 2h, 200 points." The team lead's sudowork (buyer) evaluates the proposal, counter-offers or accepts.

Protocol change: One additional field in the contract data structure:

  "initiated_by": "buyer" | "seller"

Everything after Draft is identical — the same state machine, negotiation, dual-signature, pre-commitment, and settlement flow. The only difference is who creates the Draft.

Use cases: Internal company work (proactive task proposals), open-source contributions (contributor proposes a fix, maintainer is buyer), freelance pitches (seller markets their skills to potential buyers).

5. The Escrow Problem

Points live on sudoprivacy's server. Settlement must touch the server. The question: how much power does the server have?

Option A: Server-Held Escrow

How: Buyer calls server.lock(amount, contract_hash). Server holds points until server.release() or timeout.

Pro: Seller has guarantee — points locked, buyer can't spend elsewhere.

Con: Server has unilateral release power. Buyer could hack release API. Compromised server = stolen funds. Contradicts L3 (limited trust).

Option B: Dual-Signature Settlement (no server escrow) ☆ preferred

How: No escrow. On acceptance, buyer signs a release(contract_id, amount, to_seller) message. Seller submits (release_msg + buyer_sig + seller_sig) to server's transfer API. Server verifies both signatures, executes transfer.

Pro: Server is a signature verifier, not a decider. Can't forge signatures. Can't move funds unilaterally. Minimal trust.

Con: Buyer can refuse to sign (hostage problem). Mitigated by timeout + reputation.

Option B + Pre-commitment (see below). Server as verifier aligns with L3 (limited trust).

Option B+: Buyer Pre-commitment (addresses low-trust markets)

Problem: In low-end labor markets, reputation is weak. Buyer can refuse to sign the release (hostage problem). "Timeout + reputation" is insufficient when participants have no reputation to lose.

Solution: When both parties sign the contract (agreed), buyer also signs a conditional release:

  {
    "type": "conditional_release",
    "contract_id": "...",
    "amount": 500,
    "to_seller": "seller_user_id",
    "condition": "delivery_submitted AND NOT rejected_within_timeout",
    "timeout_hours": 72,
    "buyer_signature": "sm2:..."
  }

This pre-signed message is stored on the server at contract creation time.

Settlement paths:

  • AI auto-accepts → buyer's AI checks criteria, signs explicit release → immediate transfer (no human wait)
  • AI or human rejects → must actively reject before timeout with reason → seller can revise
  • No response for 72h → server executes pre-signed conditional release automatically
  • Human async override → human can intervene at any point before timeout (local preference, e.g. "notify me for contracts >100 points")

Key insight: Server still isn't an arbiter — it only executes a commitment the buyer already signed. No unilateral power. The buyer bound themselves at contract creation time.

Clarification: The timeout_hours value is NOT set by the server. It comes from contract.terms.auto_accept_timeout_hours, which is negotiated and agreed upon by both buyer and seller before signing. The server merely reads the timeout from the buyer's pre-signed conditional release — it makes zero business decisions.

Option C: Hash-Locked Transfer (HTLC-inspired)

How: Buyer creates hash-locked commitment: "pay N points to whoever reveals preimage of H". Seller delivers + reveals preimage. Automatic.

Pro: Atomic — delivery and payment cryptographically linked.

Con: Only works for objectively verifiable deliverables (file hash). Can't handle quality verification ("labeled but poorly"). Overly complex.

Option D: Reputation-First (MVP fallback)

How: No escrow, no crypto. Buyer pays after acceptance via transfer API. Trust from reputation + signed contract history.

Pro: Simplest. No server changes. Works for trusted communities.

Con: No guarantee. Only works with social trust.

Recommendation

Start with Option B (dual-signature). It's not much harder than D, but provides real cryptographic guarantees. Server needs only one new endpoint: POST /transfer that verifies dual signatures. Option D as fallback for communities that don't need formal contracts.

Security Guarantees: why dual-sig is safe without blockchain

The protocol uses SM2 signatures (China national standard, default) + CAS content-addressing — no blockchain needed. Ed25519 available as alternative for international scenarios. Three layers of protection:

ThreatProtectionHow
Party denies signing Non-repudiation SM2/Ed25519 signature is mathematically bound to the signer's private key. "I didn't sign" is provably false.
One party acts alone Dual-sig requirement Server requires buyer_sig + seller_sig to execute any transfer. Neither party can move funds unilaterally.
Contract/records tampered CAS hash All contracts and transaction records are CAS-addressed in VFS. Change one byte → hash changes → tampering is detectable.
Server forges a transaction Server has no private keys Server cannot forge signatures. It can only verify and execute. A compromised server cannot steal funds — only fail to process (denial of service, not theft).

Trust assumption: Server will honestly execute valid transfers (L3 limited trust). If server refuses to process a valid dual-signed transfer, the signed messages serve as auditable evidence — same recourse as a bank failing to process a wire transfer.

Legal standing in China: Our SM2 dual-sig meets all four conditions of a "reliable electronic signature" under Article 14 of the Electronic Signature Law (private key is user-exclusive, user-controlled at signing, signature tamper-detectable, content tamper-detectable). Using SM2 (national cryptographic standard) rather than Ed25519 aligns with the 2025 CPS regulatory trend and may carry additional weight in compliance review. Technically valid — but in court, burden of proof is harder than CA-backed platforms (e.g. 法大大, e签宝) which have Ministry of Industry CA licenses and identity verification (ID + face recognition). Our signatures prove "this key signed this message" but not "this key belongs to Zhang San" without extra evidence. Recommendation:
  • Daily micro-tasks (¥0.30-150): Our dual-sig is sufficient. Low value → litigation probability near zero. Signed records + CAS serve as evidence chain.
  • High-value contracts (¥1000+): Optionally integrate a CA-backed e-signature API (法大大/e签宝 SaaS) as an "enhanced signing" mode. We don't need a CA license ourselves — just API integration.
  • Buyback vouchers involving cash: CA-backed signing recommended. Cash flows increase litigation risk; CA signatures have "strong evidentiary force" in Chinese courts.

5A. Reputation System (reuse from nexus)

Nexus previously had a full reputation service (deleted in PR #2600 as dead code, 759 lines). The math model is well-designed and directly reusable.

Prior art: Bayesian Beta reputation model

reputation_math.py (deleted, recoverable from git)
  • Model: Beta(alpha, beta) distribution per dimension. score = alpha / (alpha + beta). Prior Beta(1,1) = 0.5 (no information).
  • 4 dimensions: reliability (30%), quality (30%), timeliness (20%), fairness (20%) — weighted composite.
  • Time decay: Exponential with 30-day half-life. Recent events weigh more.
  • Confidence: Logarithmic scale — 0 observations = 0.0, 10 obs = 0.71, 100 obs = 0.82.
  • Federation-safe: No process-local caches. Reads go to storage directly.

Role in the system: marketplace signal, NOT settlement enforcement

Two independent mechanisms, two different problems
MechanismSolvesNature
Pre-commitment + dual-sig (§5)This transaction won't be cheatedHard guarantee (cryptographic)
Reputation (§5A)Pick a reliable counterparty next timeSoft signal (statistical)

Reputation does not participate in settlement logic. It doesn't gate payments, doesn't override pre-commitments, doesn't resolve disputes. It's a dashboard metric visible on the marketplace — helps buyers choose sellers and sellers choose buyers. The two systems are intentionally decoupled.

Bilateral: both buyer AND seller are rated

DimensionRating SellerRating Buyer
Reliability (30%)Delivers on time, doesn't ghostResponds to delivery on time, doesn't ghost
Quality (30%)Deliverable meets acceptance criteriaRequirements are clear and fair
Timeliness (20%)Completes before deadlineAccepts/rejects within timeout
Fairness (20%)Reasonable pricing, honest workDoesn't maliciously reject, doesn't underpay

Buyer reputation matters as much as seller reputation — sellers need to know if a buyer is trustworthy before accepting a task. Same model as ride-sharing (driver rates passenger, passenger rates driver).

Each event is CAS-addressed — tamper-evident. Composite score is recomputable from events.

Agent rates on behalf of human (L1 alignment)

The agent submits feedback on behalf of its human. This is not autonomous AI scoring — the agent acts within the L1 trust zone, aligned with its human's interests (the human paid for the points powering the agent).

How much the human reviews vs delegates to the AI is a local preference: some users will review every rating, others will let their AI handle routine feedback. This alignment process is entirely within L1 — out of protocol scope. The protocol only requires that feedback is signed by the agent's key, which the human controls.

Reuse reputation_math.py pure functions (Beta scoring + time decay + composite). Replace ORM with VFS JSON. Bilateral rating (buyer + seller). Reputation is a marketplace signal only — does not gate settlement.

Portable Credentials: from reputation to employment

China has ~11.7M university graduates per year (2025) and record-low youth employment rates. AI skill-building alone helps, but students need verifiable proof that employers recognize. The reputation system provides exactly this — a tamper-evident, employer-verifiable work history.

Two tiers of certification (potential partnership with Ministry of Education contacts):

TierCriteriaWhat it proves
AI Work Proficiency (通用) Complete N tasks across any category, composite reputation > threshold, sustained over M months This person can collaborate with AI tools to produce professional-quality output — prompt crafting, AI-assisted execution, quality verification.
Domain Specialist (垂直) Complete N tasks in a specific domain (e.g. video editing, data labeling, code review), domain quality score > threshold This person is a verified practitioner in [domain], with quantified quality metrics from real commercial work.

Why this is better than traditional certificates: A paper certificate says "this institution vouches for this person." Our credential says "this person completed 200 video editing tasks for real buyers, with a mean quality score of 0.92, verified by CAS-addressed task records that any employer can audit." The proof is in the work history, not the issuer's reputation.

The full loop: Student receives $100 seed tokens → completes tasks → earns points + learns AI skills + builds reputation → earns portable credential → credential helps land a job. The platform doesn't just provide income during university — it builds a verifiable career asset that outlasts the platform itself.

Social context: Youth unemployment is a pressing societal issue in China. A platform that simultaneously provides (a) income, (b) AI skill development, and (c) employer-recognized credentials addresses all three dimensions. The Ministry of Education partnership would lend institutional legitimacy, while the on-chain work history provides substance that traditional credentials lack.

Stress tests:

  • Why students over Fiverr freelancers?: (a) Token leverage — students get 10-20x AI value, Fiverr freelancers get cash only. (b) sudowork handles the hard part (editing), student handles the easy part (creative review). (c) Campus distribution = low CAC.

Scenario C: Social Media Engagement (Douyin)

Buyer: Brand/creator wants organic engagement. "Visit my Douyin profile, browse 3+ videos, follow if interested, like 1-2 videos, leave a genuine comment. 10 points."

Seller: Real person with a Douyin account. Their sudowork guides them through the task.

Acceptance criteria:

  • Profile visited (screenshot or interaction proof)
  • ≥3 videos watched (view duration proof)
  • Follow action completed (follower count delta or screenshot)
  • 1-2 likes placed
  • 1 genuine comment left (not spam, contextually relevant)

Delivery: Interaction proof bundle (screenshots, timestamps) in VFS.

Stress tests:

  • Proof of action: How to prove the engagement happened? Screenshots can be faked. Need to think about attestation — perhaps browser automation logs from sudowork's ai-dev-browser?
  • Micro-task economics: 10 points per task = very small. Need efficient contract negotiation (templates?) and low-friction settlement. Can't have heavyweight crypto for micro-payments.
Design implications from scenarios
  • Mixed verification: Protocol must support both machine-verifiable and human-judged criteria, explicitly marked in the contract.
  • Proof types: Different tasks need different proof mechanisms — CAS hash for data files, screenshots/logs for actions. Protocol should be proof-type-agnostic.
  • Template contracts: Low-value tasks (Scenario C) need lightweight contracts. Template-based "task types" skip negotiation for standard tasks.
  • Scenario-dependent economics: Video editing (Scenario A) is cash-competitive. Lower-value scenarios depend on token leverage. Protocol is the same; business model adapts per scenario.

At ¥80-150 per video, the combined AI cost for buyer and seller is under ¥1.50 — less than 1% of the task payment. Both buyer and seller economics are strong without needing token leverage. This is the fundamental difference from micro-tasks: the task payment is high enough that AI assistance is essentially free.

2. Seller's AI Investment

The seller's AI tokens are the seller's own investment, not the platform's cost. How much AI help to use is a local optimization within the seller's L1 trust zone:

  • High-skill seller: Does most editing manually, uses AI for captions and format checks only (saves tokens, keeps more profit)
  • Low-skill seller: Relies heavily on sudowork's editing skill — template selection, auto-captioning, music matching, pacing (spends more tokens, but produces professional output they couldn't achieve alone)
  • Model choice: Each seller picks their own model. Simple format checks → DSV4. Creative suggestions → may use a stronger model. The protocol doesn't mandate a model.

4. Token Economy

$100 seed tokens for university students

Distribution via university partnerships: each student receives $100 free tokens (≈ 10,000 points) upon registration. This seeds the flywheel — students use tokens to run sudowork's editing skill, complete tasks, earn more points, and eventually become micro-buyers who dispatch sub-tasks (caption translation, thumbnail design) to other students. Tokens circulate within the student network, creating an organic P2P economy. Aligns with the BP's "¥100/day free token" strategy but anchored to a specific task type with clear ROI.

10-20x leverage: use AI vs cash out

The buyback rate (5-10% of original charge price) creates a 10-20x leverage effect that naturally drives AI adoption:

Seller choicePoints valueEffective valueMultiplier
Use points for AI (sudowork) 50 points from task ¥0.50 worth of AI compute 1x face value
Cash out (buyback) 50 points from task ¥0.025-0.05 cash 0.05-0.1x face value

The economics: Model provider subsidies (e.g. DSV4 pricing) mean the platform's actual compute cost is far below the charge rate. This cost gap is passed to sellers as amplified AI value — 50 points buys ¥0.50 of AI compute that costs the platform perhaps ¥0.05. The buyback rate roughly matches the platform's real cost, so it's sustainable.

Buyback mechanism

Sellers earn points but need real currency. sudowork offers a stable-price buyback at 5-10% of the original charge price.

  • Framing: "refund" or "credit redemption" — not "exchange" or "withdrawal" (avoids financial instrument classification)
  • Mechanism: Seller requests buyback → sudoprivacy credits the amount to seller's payment method at the published buyback rate
  • Rate example: Charge rate ¥1 = 100 points. Buyback rate: 100 points = ¥0.05-0.10
  • Policy risk: Needs legal review. Framing as "partial refund of unused credits" rather than "currency exchange" is safer. Precedent: Steam Wallet, Apple App Store credits have similar refund mechanisms.

Secondary market: buyback vouchers

Tokens have real economic value (model APIs, skills, SaaS) — a secondary market will form naturally, whether we facilitate it or not (same as game currency, Steam balance, etc.). Unmanaged off-platform trading is risky (fraud) and invisible (no data). To keep the secondary market within our visibility while staying out of regulatory scope, we introduce buyback vouchers:

  1. A (buyer of tokens) deposits ¥X with sudoprivacy → receives a buyback voucher worth ¥X (100% buyback, but cannot be used for AI or any service — it is purely a refund right)
  2. A transfers the voucher to B (via dual-sig) ↔ B transfers tokens to A (via dual-sig)
  3. B presents the voucher to sudoprivacy → receives ¥X cash

The exchange rate between voucher and tokens is negotiated by A and B — the platform does not set or suggest a rate. A might pay ¥50 voucher for 70 tokens (B gets ¥50 cash for 70 tokens = 71% rate), or any other ratio they agree on.

Design constraintRequirementWhy
Voucher cannot buy AI/servicesYesDistinguishes it from virtual currency — it's a prepaid refund right
Voucher single-transfer onlyYesA → B only, no further circulation → not a tradable instrument
Voucher amount = deposit amountYes100% buyback, no financial derivative characteristics
Platform does not set exchange rateYesAvoids "exchange" or "marketplace" regulatory classification
All transfers recorded by serverAlready doneDual-sig transfers (§5) already give server full visibility — no extra infrastructure needed

What this achieves: Sellers get a better cash-out rate than 5% buyback (market-determined, likely 20-40%). Buyers get tokens at face value (no discount). Platform has full visibility into secondary market flows without participating as an intermediary. No new regulatory exposure — vouchers are prepaid refund rights (similar to 7-day unconditional refund under 2025 Supreme Court prepaid consumption interpretation), not virtual currency.

Regulatory note: This mechanism requires legal review before implementation. The key defense is that vouchers are (a) non-functional (can't buy services), (b) non-circulating (single transfer), (c) fixed-value (100% of deposit). These properties distinguish them from virtual currency under the 2026 eight-ministry circular. Nevertheless, the substance-over-form doctrine means regulators may look at the overall flow (A pays cash → B receives cash → tokens move) regardless of labeling.

Scenario framing

For Scenario A (video editing), cash income alone is competitive — ¥160-300/h exceeds all student alternatives. Token leverage is a bonus that amplifies already-strong economics. For lower-value scenarios (Scenario C: Douyin micro-tasks at ¥0.30-0.50/task), token leverage becomes the primary differentiator — the 10-20x AI value gap is what makes the gig worth doing.

7. Seller Specialization

Sellers who accumulate task history in a specific domain can build a reputation moat through post-training. A seller with local GPU (e.g. DGX) can fine-tune a model on their completed task data — labeled images, edited video patterns, code review decisions — increasing quality and speed over time. Higher quality → higher reputation → more tasks at higher prices (positive feedback loop). For sellers without GPU, sudowork's model choice meta-learning remembers which model works best for which task type and which seller, providing lightweight specialization for everyone.

8. Open Questions

  1. Escrow model: Start with B (dual-signature) or D (reputation)?
    Start with B. The server API cost is one endpoint (POST /transfer with signature verification). Reputation alone (D) is too weak for strangers. B gives real guarantees from day one.
  2. Dispute resolution: P2P reputation or platform arbitration?
    P2P reputation for v1. Platform arbitration is a v2 feature that requires sudoprivacy to build an arbitration team/process. Signed contracts + audit logs provide evidence if disputes escalate outside the system.
  3. Points transfer API: Does sudoprivacy already support user-to-user transfers?
    Confirmed: no transfer API exists. Current system is charge (pay sudoprivacy) and consume (use LLM) only. Need new endpoint: POST /api/v1/transfer with dual-signature verification. The transfer must accept (release_msg + buyer_sig + seller_sig), verify both SM2 signatures against the contract terms, and atomically move points between accounts. Minimal surface — one new endpoint, no escrow state.
  4. Discovery / Marketplace: How do buyers find sellers?
    v1: KOOK community server for discovery + direct P2P for settlement. Buyers post task needs in a KOOK channel, sellers see and initiate contract negotiation via sudowork. KOOK is free for server creation, messaging, and bots — no marketplace rake, no hidden fees (VIP only for voice features like screen sharing and AI noise reduction). KOOK does not participate in settlement — it's a discovery channel only. Evolution path: KOOK community → sudowork built-in task board (simple list view) → full marketplace (search + recommendation + seller profiles). Each step gated by PMF signal. Risk: if KOOK charges for commercial use later, migration cost is low (Feishu group / Telegram group are drop-in replacements). Self-built marketplace is a significant build (search, profiles, taxonomy) — premature for v1.
  5. Points buyback mechanism: How do sellers convert earned points to real currency?
    Stable-price buyback at 5-10% of original charge price (see §7A). Key open items: (a) legal classification — "partial refund" framing vs "currency exchange" risk, needs legal review; (b) buyback rate stability — fixed rate or floating with usage metrics; (c) minimum threshold — avoid micro-withdrawals that cost more to process than they're worth; (d) frequency limits — daily/weekly caps to manage cash flow. Policy risk is real: if regulators classify points as a financial instrument, compliance burden increases significantly. Precedent: Steam Wallet, Apple App Store credits have similar refund mechanisms.
